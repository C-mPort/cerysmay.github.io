In the present, zoos are one of the most popular days out whilst supporting conservation. But upon learning how many animals in zoos are not endangered, many have questions as to why and whether zoos are helping the cause as much as is claimed.

A Basic Breakdown of Zoo Finances

Animal rights activists have complained that zoos finances aren’t transparent or detailed enough. So how does it work?

It is important to understand that not all zoos in the UK and Ireland operate under the same business model. While most zoos are registered charities, 45% of zoos are privately owned. However, many zoos combine business and charity so they can keep the conservation work they do separate to the cost for upkeep on zoos.

For example, Chester zoo is a charity managed by the North England Zoological Society which has businesses underneath. Though these operate as businesses, the profits go back into the charity which covers the cost of running the zoo and funds conservation efforts around the globe.

Zoos make their money mostly through ticket sales, memberships, and on-site spending for food and drink, parking, and gift shop sales. For most zoos, the average admission cost is between £20 to £25, although this can climb to almost £50 in some places with donations added on top.

Zoos also make money through experiences, typically animal feedings or zookeeper for a day. These experiences usually give a behind-the-scenes look at taking care of the animals and the processes involved in being a zookeeper. These experiences usually last a full day and cost around £250 to £300 and are targeted at older teens and adults.

But what does it cost to run a zoo? There are expenses that many would assume such as cost of food, water, activities and stimulation for animals, veterinary costs, and more general costs such as lights, water, pay for the staff who keep the zoo running, and maintenance for state-of-the-art enclosures and habitats.

Such costs add up for zoos, with the BBC finding that 40% of accredited zoos and aquariums in the UK and Ireland have raised concerns around finances publicly since 2022.

There are many costs that cannot be cut for zoos, especially surrounding keeping animals healthy and happy. However, many zoos across the UK are working towards using completely sustainable off-the-grid energy.

Zoos like Marwell zoo which create their own renewable energy using animal waste and Edinburgh Zoo who have a 5000 square meter solar meadow that creates almost a quarter of the zoo’s electricity pave the way in cutting down costs and sustainability efforts.

One of the biggest examples of a charity based zoo in the UK is Chester Zoo. As they are a registered charity, the income and expenditures of the zoo are publicly available and provide some insight into the income streams and what money is spent on.

The North England Zoological Society, the charity branch of Chester zoo, brought in just over £63 million pounds in 2024 through four main income streams, the largest of which was charitable activities which brought in £38 million, a category that includes income generated through the activities of Chester Zoo, such as admissions and educational programmes as well as the one government grant given to Chester zoo at £201,000.

Just over £60 million of the money made by the North England Zoological Society goes into expenditure, with almost £35 million going back into the fundraising category – though this covers running site through animal care and maintenance as well as funding developments and fundraising. Just over £26 million going back into charitable activities such as research, education and conservation programmes.

Out of the almost 670 employees under the North England Zoological Society, only 24 make over £60,000 a year as of 2024. Half of these employees make £60 - £70,000 a year.

Zoos argue that the visitor income supports more than just the animals currently held in captivity. These funds not only support veterinary care and enriching the lives of the animals, but it also supports conservation research, educational programmes, national and international field projects, and breeding programmes. Many zoos in the UK are also members of the BIAZA and contribute to the association's charitable work as well.

Despite this, animal rights activists argue that this money would be better placed elsewhere. When talking to a spokesperson for the international animal rights organisation PETA, they said that expensive and long-drawn projects divert money from other causes.

“The massively expensive breeding programmes divert money from genuine in situ wildlife conservation projects.”

This is along with the criticism that a lot of zoos, especially those operated as companies, do not have to be transparent with where money goes. Though these zoos, especially accredited zoos do contribute to conservation through their accreditation membership and programmes they run, there is a lacking level of transparency.

Ultimately, deciding on whether the money you spend at a zoo goes towards conservation depends on what zoo you are at. At some zoos, it is not possible for the public to determine from the available financial information exactly how income is allocated. As public interest in conservation continues to grow, the calls for more transparent declarations into where money is spent in zoos increases.
