The New Modern Zoo Standards

Indoor space for elephants will have to be dramatically increased by 2040 in the eleven zoos across the UK that keep Asian and African Elephants, according to new regulations for zoos in Britain.

Though zoo licensing Is set out in the 1981 Zoo Licensing Act, the inspections and requirements are set in the Standards of Modern Zoos. For the first time in almost 15 years for Scotland and England and 20 years for Wales, these standards are set to change in 2027 with the release of the Standards of Modern Zoo Practice 2026.

One of the biggest changes in the UK standards comes with specific guidelines for specific species, the largest of these changes being standards for keeping elephants which zoos will need to put in place by 2040.

The new enclosure standards require significantly increased space for each elephant that will need to be done by 2040. The original 2012 Scottish and English requirements for indoor spaces required 200 square meters for four or fewer adult individuals and 80 square meters for each additional adult. Facilities for males if housed alone require at least 80 square meters and the height to ensure they are comfortable.

The new indoor enclosures will see large changes, as the size for female enclosures triple from 200 to 600 square meters for four or fewer animals over the age of 2. In the 2012 standards, if there were more than four over 2 years old, the indoor enclosure size had to increase by 80 square metres. Now, with the new Standards, it will have to increase by 100 square metres for each new member.

In some zoos, males are housed separately indoors from the rest of a female herd or from other males to stop fighting or unwanted breeding particularly during Musth: the reproduction period in adult male elephants. The original 2012 standards said that the stall size for individual housing of bulls must be at least 80 square metres. With the new standards, this has quadrupled – zoos are now required to provide at least 320 square metres for individual bulls.

Even bigger changes are in store for the outdoor enclosures. The 2012 standards for Scotland and England require at least 2000 square metres for eight or more females over two years old, increasing to 3000 if there is a male in the herd. With the new standards coming into practice in 2040, all outdoor enclosures with eight or fewer individuals will require 20,000 square meters regardless of sex of the individual animals.

The amount of space for each additional individual the standard is also increasing dramatically. In the 2012 standards, outdoor space was required to increase by 200 square metres for every animal over the 8 individuals who are 2 years or older. In the new standards, the space must increase by 2,500 for every individual over 5 who are 2 years or older.

Despite these new changes, animal rights activists still believe that these are not enough. While the new rules do provide an increased need for animal care with extra enrichment and space Chris Lewis from Born Free said that there are concerns about how other animals such as birds are kept and whether the space and enrichment for them is enough.

"If you see how birds are kept and the freedom they have compared to the wild, there is a big ethical question there," he said.

The biggest concerns raised by Lewis though was what Born Free has dubbed, ‘zoothanasia’, which they define on their website as:

"Where zoos routinely kill healthy individuals that are no longer useful or profitable and are considered 'surplus stock'

In zoos, this is commonly called management euthanasia and is a practice most common in European zoos if an individual cannot fit with the conservation or breeding plans of a zoo.

Though this concern is raised about zoos in the UK and Ireland, speaking to a spokesperson at Folly Farm revealed that the practice is not very common in the UK. He said in the UK, zoos tend to use other options such as keeping bachelor groups like Folly Farm’s own group of giraffes, birth control in certain species, moving animals between zoos, and putting animals into re-introduction programmes.

However, this does raise questions around how zoos handle the reporting of animal deaths. Across the UK, Ireland, and Europe, animal deaths are required to be reported, however, the cause of death is not.

Many zoos track causes of death on zoo management systems and do perform post-mortems to investigate why an animal has died, however, there is no obligation to publish these findings. Animal rights activists are concerned that deaths caused by accident, neglect, or management euthanasia could go under or completely unreported due to the lack of requirements to report them publicly.

The debate surrounding the welfare of animals kept in zoos comes down to whether the benefits of captivity outweigh the limitations of keeping an animal from its natural habitat. While critics argue that needs are not being met, zoos argue that they are the best solution and are trying to improve constantly.
