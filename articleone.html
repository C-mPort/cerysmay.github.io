How Zoos Play In the World of Conservation

For decades, animal rights activists have ccampaigned against zoos, claiming they do little to benefit conservation internationally. Almost half of the animals in UK and Irish zoos aren't in danger of dying out, which may surprise some of the 30 million people who visit accredited zoos each year.

Zoos however argue that it is difficult to describe the full scope of the work they do, and say that keeping these animals helps to preserve them if they become at risk of exintction in the future and creates a healthy breeding population

Chris Lewis from Born Free, a UK based animal rights charity, said one of the reasons animals are kept is to entertain and attract visitors. "There is usually a role along the lines of visitor engagement to appeal to the public" he says. Animals that are in the conservation category of "Least Concern" in terms of extinction, "are popular with the public" he said.

Lewis also said that keeping species that are endangered in zoos may confuse or distract visitors, making them believe the animals are not endangered.

"Sometimes keeping species in zoos distracts. They see a species in a zoo then think that because they are in a zoo they are okay and protected."

Lewis suggests zoos are best equipped for housing smaller animals like reptiles, invertebrates, and amphibians; as well as species native to the country the zoo is based as these animals can be provided enclosures that are closer to their natural habitat.

For mammals, he says "the populations aren't large enough to be genetically diverse in many species. Or even in prey species, they don't have the survival behavioural traits they would need."

Rhian Waller, from Chester Zoo, says conservation from a zoo's perspective is complicated. She said that many of the more publicised sides of conservation such as species being reintroduced can take decades.

"Species reintroductions and landscape restorations can take decades to come to furition, and the planning and co-ordination that goes into the work is phenomenal.

Both Waller and Lewis sadi conservation can be poorly explained. Similarly, a spokesperson from Folly Farm, a Welsh family-run zoo and farm said that the public misunderstands, "the sheer amount of conservation that zoos carry out", and that it is, "often difficult to communicate what conservation work is being carried out in detail".

Both zoos said how important education is to the goal of zoos and conservation: about endangered species and species that are under-recognised. Chester Zoo's event Action Indonesia Day, in August this year, taught visitors about Indonesian species like the anoa, babirusa, and the banteng.

Waller said that non-endangered animals are kept in zoos beccause of the public interest but once people are in the zoo they find out about other lesser-known and potentially more at-risk animals.

"They are essentially animals that help us drive funda towards under-recognised species and engage people with more holistic conservation messaging."

Waller also clarified that while many species in zoos are not currently at risk of extinction, many species are facing significant threats in the wild. By keeping a population of these species in captivity, it ensures if a species does later become at risk of extinction that a healthy and genetically diverse breeding population is available.

"While many species are classified as Least Concern right now, global biodiversity is trending downward... by the time a species becomes endangered, the genetic diversity of the population has already significantly reduced."

Almost half the species in UK zoos have decreasing populations and are over two times as likely to have a decreasing population. For example, the Plains Zebra was deemed Near Threatened in 2016 after a significant decline in the global population caused by an increase in illegal poaching.

Their population decreased from over 670,000 in 1992 down to just under 500,000 in 2015 when they were listed as Near Threatened by the IUCN, the global environmental network which tracks the populations of fauna and flora.

The white rhinoceros is the most common type of rhinoceros in the world and has been hit by similar decline due to illegal poaching, leading to dramatic fluctuations in the species number over recent years.

Although in recent years there has been some recovery, numbers dropped from just over 17,00 in 2023 to under 15,800 in 2024 - their lowest point since the early 2010s.

Though both animal rights activists and zoos argue around the best ways to support conservation, both agree on certain cases where animals must be kept in captivity.

The siz BIAZA zoos examined including Chester Zoo and Folly Farm house eleven animals which are extinct in the wild. Nine of these are various types of Partula snail or tree snail. The remaining two are the Socorro dove and Pere David's deer.

These species require the most support, and their status in captivity protects them from extinction. "If a species is extinct in the wild, there is justification as to why that animal is kept in captivity, but there has to be an action plan to release them", Lewis said.

Chester Zoo is currently involved in one of these action plans for snails which are Extinct in the Wild. They have been working for several years with governments to restore habitats and clear invasive species from uninhabited Atlantic islands so these snails can be released back into the islands' ecosystem.
